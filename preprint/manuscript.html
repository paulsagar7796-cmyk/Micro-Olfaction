Abstract

Metal-oxide gas sensor arrays drift, so a classifier trained on early recordings loses accuracy over the following months. Drift-compensation methods published for the UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift dataset typically adapt in batch or offline, often using the whole target batch, and none of those compared here can adapt within a microcontroller's memory. We evaluate a lightweight, resource-aware online method, SOLAR+ (Streaming Online Linear Alignment and Readout): running alignment of 256 features, a linear logistic readout trained by stochastic gradient descent, strong calibration on a few labelled samples per gas, a class weight, and streaming pseudo-labels. All method choices were made on Batches 2–5; Batches 6–10 (months 17–36) are held out. With 20 balanced target-calibration samples, SOLAR+ exceeds the published DA-ELM result by 4.3 percentage points in both standard evaluation settings, and at 50 samples it comes within 1–4 points (and exceeds it in Setting 2). The advantage depends on balanced calibration: with DA-ELM's gas-agnostic label selection, SOLAR+ trails at every labelled budget, and without target labels TDACNN remains ahead. A C implementation reproduces the method's specification bit for bit. On an emulated Cortex-M4F its inference takes 27,666 cycles. Its adaptation runs entirely on-chip within 59,780 bytes of state, whereas DA-ELM requires at least 4 MB of memory and cannot adapt without offloading to a gateway or server. A concentration-level hold-out reduces few-shot accuracy by 7–11 percentage points, indicating that conventional benchmark results can benefit from concentration-level proximity, although calibration from other concentration levels retains 47–67 % of the accuracy gain. On independent sensor boards, SOLAR+ achieves 95.4 % cross-board accuracy after calibration on 20 of each board's trials and raises the weakest board from 58 % to 90 %; a random forest given the same calibration trials reaches 98.9 %. We also document a contradiction in the dataset's gas naming. Code, verification scripts, and raw experimental artifacts are archived for reproducibility.

Introduction

Metal-oxide (MOx) gas sensors are inexpensive and sensitive, but their responses drift over months, so a classifier trained on early recordings gradually loses accuracy . Collecting a new labelled training set whenever this happens is costly. Drift compensation instead adapts an existing classifier to the drifted data. The UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift dataset, recorded over 36 months with an array of 16 MOx sensors , is its standard benchmark.

Many of the methods evaluated on this benchmark adapt in batch. DA-ELM computes the output weights of an extreme learning machine in closed form, from a stored labelled source batch and a few labelled target samples chosen from the whole target batch; its transductive variant also uses the whole unlabelled target batch. ODAELM makes the update online but maintains a 1,000 × 1,000 inverse matrix. TDACNN and AMDS-PFFA are deep neural networks trained offline. An electronic nose, however, often runs on an edge microcontroller with a few hundred kilobytes of memory and a constrained battery budget. Its samples arrive sequentially, and labelled samples come from occasional maintenance visits with reference gases.

We ask how far a lightweight, resource-aware online method gets under these hardware constraints, and at what computational cost. The method, SOLAR+ (Figure ), standardises each feature with running statistics, classifies with six linear logistic units, and adapts at each maintenance visit by calibrating on a few labelled samples per gas; between visits it can update itself on confident predictions. Its whole state, including the labelled samples it adapts from, fits within microcontroller SRAM. Our contributions are:

  • Accuracy under matched label budgets. With every method choice made on Batches 2–5 and Batches 6–10 held out, SOLAR+ with 20 balanced target-calibration samples exceeds the published DA-ELM result by 4.3 percentage points in both standard evaluation settings (section ). We also report where it loses: with DA-ELM's gas-agnostic label selection, without target labels, and against a multi-source method.
  • Microcontroller-scale memory and adaptation. A C implementation, bit-identical to the method's specification, runs on an emulated Cortex-M4F in 27,666 cycles per inference. Adaptation executes entirely on-chip within 59,780 bytes of state, whereas DA-ELM requires at least 4 MB of working memory and must offload to a server (section ).
  • A check of benchmark optimism. The dataset has no timestamps, and within most batch files a sample's position predicts its gas. A concentration-level hold-out reduces few-shot accuracy by 7–11 percentage points, indicating that conventional benchmark results benefit from concentration-level proximity (section ).
  • Validation across sensor boards. On independent physical sensor boards, SOLAR+ achieves 95.4 % cross-board accuracy and lifts the performance on the most degraded board from 58 % to 90 % (section ).
  • Gas naming and reproducibility. We document a contradiction in the dataset's documentation mapping class numbers to gases (section ). All code, verification scripts, and check routines are archived, reproducing every figure and table from raw data (section ).

Related work

Drift compensation on this dataset. Vergara et al. recorded the benchmark dataset and compensated drift with a weighted ensemble of support vector machines trained at different times. Zhang and Zhang proposed domain adaptation extreme learning machines: DAELM-S learns from the stored labelled source batch and a few labelled target samples, and DAELM-T from the labelled target samples, the source classifier, and the whole unlabelled target batch. Their two evaluation settings, training on Batch 1 or on the preceding batch, are the standard protocols (section ). Ma et al. introduced an online variant (ODAELM) with a Woodbury update per sample. TDACNN is a convolutional network that requires no target data, and AMDS-PFFA is an attention-based network that adapts from two labelled source batches to the whole unlabelled target batch. Section compares SOLAR+ with all four under matched label budgets.

Label selection. DA-ELM chooses its labelled target samples by farthest-point sampling over the whole target batch , and ODAELM's offline variant uses Kennard-Stone selection , which also scans the entire batch in advance. A maintenance visit instead presents reference gases sequentially, yielding labels balanced across gases in arrival order. We evaluate both modes of selection.

Benchmark validity. Dennler et al. demonstrated on a wind-tunnel dataset that temporal clustering lets classifiers exploit baseline sensor states before exposure. The drift dataset provides no timestamps and no baseline measured before each exposure, precluding their exact test; section evaluates what can be verified empirically. Rodriguez-Lujan et al. annotated each measurement's concentration, enabling our concentration-level hold-out analysis.

Data and gas naming

Dataset. The UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift dataset contains 13,910 measurements of six gases across various concentrations, collected with 16 MOx sensors in 10 batches over 36 months (Table ). Each measurement contains 128 features (eight per sensor): steady-state resistance change, normalised steady-state change, and exponential-moving-average extrema across three smoothing constants for rising and decaying transients . We use the concentration-annotated extension , whose feature matrices match the original. A download script verifies file checksums and per-batch sample counts against UCI's published repository tables.

Gas naming contradiction. UCI's documentation provides two contradictory mappings between class numbers and gas names (Table ). The coding sentence describes the order shown in the right column. Conversely, the published sample-count tables in Vergara et al. , Zhang and Zhang (Table I), and subsequent literature match the middle column. The text in the coding sentence is identical to the column-header order of the sample-count table, suggesting it was transcribed directly from headers. Although documented concentration ranges align slightly better with the coding sentence, response-strength comparisons remain inconclusive. To maintain direct comparability with published benchmark figures, we adopt the count-table naming while explicitly reporting class numbers throughout.

Method

SOLAR+ extends a baseline pipeline, SOLAR (Streaming Online Linear Alignment and Readout), with three components tuned exclusively on the validation batches (Figure ). In the accompanying codebase, these pipelines are designated internally as System 2 and System 2+. Each sample is predicted without access to its label or any future sample; the current unlabeled observation may update the running alignment statistics before prediction.

Features

The three decaying-transient features of each sensor are negative, so their signs are inverted: larger responses yield strictly larger positive values across all features. SOLAR appends 128 log-pattern features to these 128 sign-corrected values. For each of the eight feature types, sensor values are floored at 10⁻³ of Batch 1's median magnitude, transformed via natural logarithm, and centered by subtracting the across-sensor mean of these logarithms. Common sensor-gain shifts cancel in this representation, presenting 256 inputs to the classifier.

Running alignment

Each feature x is standardised using running first and second moments updated on arrival: μ ← αμ + (1 − α)x, σ² ← ασ² + (1 − α)(x − μ)², with aligned score z = (x − μ)/√(σ² + ε) at smoothing parameter α = 0.995. At the onset of each target batch in Settings 1 and 2, statistics are initialized to the source batch's empirical mean and variance. This alignment operates entirely unsupervised.

Readout

Six one-vs-rest logistic units read out the aligned features, predicting the gas with the maximal score. Initial training on the source batch uses scikit-learn's SGDClassifier with log loss, L2 regularisation parameter λ = 10⁻⁵, and scikit-learn's "optimal" learning-rate schedule. Subsequent online learning applies incremental SGD steps along the continued step-size schedule.

Calibration at maintenance visits

At each target batch, a scheduled maintenance visit provides labelled calibration samples. Samples pass through running alignment, after which the readout performs 50 passes of SGD with shuffled presentation orders. SOLAR processes these with unit loss weight; SOLAR+ applies a loss multiplier of K = 3 (strong calibration). Calibration samples are never evaluated in scoring.

Class weight

SOLAR+ weights class 4 (ethylene) by a factor of 2 in the loss across source training and subsequent adaptation, selected from {1.5, 2, 3, 4} during validation.

Pseudo-labels

As the target batch streams in, SOLAR+ predicts each sample. If the normalised winning probability across all six units meets or exceeds 0.95, an SGD update is taken on the predicted label with loss weighted by 0.1, capped at 50 pseudo-labels per gas per batch. Prediction strictly precedes any update triggered by that observation.

Model selection

All pipeline parameters (features, regularisation, calibration epochs, and alignment decay) were fixed on Batches 2–5. SOLAR+'s components were selected on Batches 2–5 across Settings 1 and 2, tuning K over {1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300}. Batches 6–10 were held out. A non-linear random projection layer (64–1,024 units, ReLU or tanh) was tested and rejected on validation: its top variant achieved 84.9 % versus 85.7 % for the linear readout while increasing inference complexity by 6–97×.

Evaluation protocols

Settings. Setting 1 trains on Batch 1 and evaluates each Batch K independently; Setting 2 trains on Batch K−1 and tests on Batch K . A multi-source setup (Batches 1 + 2 → K) is evaluated against AMDS-PFFA.

Labels. Target batches receive 0, 20, 30, or 50 labelled calibration samples, which are excluded from accuracy metrics. Balanced calibration distributes samples equally per gas from the initial streaming arrivals, mimicking reference-cylinder calibration. DAELM selection replicates the farthest-point batch sampling described in Zhang and Zhang .

Streams. The order of samples within each batch is permuted with fixed seeds to eliminate sorting artifacts from file indices (section ).

Scoring. Reported accuracy represents the unweighted mean of per-batch test accuracies on Batches 6–10 (months 17–36). Values report mean ± standard deviation across 5 seeds varying calibration sampling and training order. Baseline metrics are transcribed from Tables II–III of , Tables 2–3 of , Tables 4–5 of , and Table III of (arXiv v1).

Sequential maintenance protocol. To model deployment on physical hardware, an unreset sequential protocol trains on 80 % of Batch 1 and processes Batches 2–10 sequentially. Alignment moments, weights, and pseudo-label updates persist across batches with periodic calibration, evaluated over 15 seeds with 5 to 15 labels per gas.

Results

Accuracy against DA-ELM

Table displays accuracy across Batches 6–10. With 20 balanced target-calibration samples, SOLAR+ exceeds the published DA-ELM result by 4.3 percentage points in both standard evaluation settings (+4.3 in Setting 1 and +4.3 in Setting 2). At 30 labels, differences are −1.5 (Setting 1) and +1.9 (Setting 2); at 50 labels, −3.6 and +1.7. Because seed variation spans ±1–4 points and published baselines report no variance, the Setting 2 lead at 50 labels is not statistically definitive. Under DA-ELM's gas-agnostic sampling, SOLAR+ trails published figures by 0.9–7.2 points, demonstrating that its comparative advantage relies on balanced reference calibration.

SOLAR+ outperforms SOLAR by 2.9 percentage points averaged across all settings, budgets, and selection protocols. Class 4 (ethylene) recall increases from 41.1 % to 52.6 %, lifting macro recall from 74.9 % to 77.9 %. Earlier baselines reported in Zhang and Zhang (Batches 2–10 means): Setting 1 — SVM-rbf 38.9, CC-PCA 45.7, SVM-comgfk 64.0, ML-comgfk 67.3; Setting 2 — SVM-rbf 68.0, SVM-comgfk 69.4, ML-comgfk 79.6, Vergara ensemble 80.0.

Ablation

Table provides incremental ablations. Strong calibration provides the primary performance boost. Excessive calibration loss weights degrade generalisation: validation accuracy drops from 87.8 % at K = 3 to 82.9 % at K = 300.

Comparison with post-2015 methods

Table benchmarks SOLAR+ against subsequent literature on Batches 6–10, recomputing plain per-batch means directly from reported per-batch numbers.

  • Zero labels: TDACNN leads the zero-label setting, outperforming SOLAR+ by 8.3 points in Setting 1 and 7.3 points in Setting 2. AMDS-PFFA exceeds our multi-source run by 22.1 points at 0 labels and 6.7 points at 20 labels.
  • With target labels: Published post-2015 methods do not surpass DA-ELM or SOLAR+ under matched label budgets. ODAELM-S offline with 50 Kennard-Stone labels scores 79.7 % (counting calibration samples as correct predictions), trailing SOLAR+ with DAELM selection at 86.1 %.
  • Online comparison: ODAELM-S online achieves 84.4 % (with calibration samples scored). Under causal streaming with non-scored calibration, SOLAR+ reaches 86.8 % at 30 and 88.7 % at 50 balanced labels.

Performance boundaries

SOLAR+ is competitive with published drift-compensation methods under the evaluated protocols once balanced calibration labels are supplied. In the strict zero-label regime (no target labels and no target feature streaming), TDACNN performs strongest, scoring 64.7 % in Setting 1 and 72.7 % in Setting 2 versus 56.4 % and 65.4 % for uncalibrated SOLAR+. AMDS-PFFA achieves 81.3 % at zero target labels, though it requires multi-source training and full target-batch transductive adaptation.

Sequential maintenance and pseudo-labels

In the sequential protocol over Batches 6–10 (15 seeds), SOLAR+ achieves 91.5 %, 92.7 %, and 93.7 % accuracy at 10, 12, and 15 labels per gas (SOLAR: 90.0 %, 91.3 %, 92.3 %), and 88.6 % at 5 labels. Pseudo-label precision ranges between 84 % and 100 % across batches, providing a stable +0.3 to +0.6 point increment without runaway divergence across nine consecutive recalibration cycles.

Low-confidence pseudo-label dynamics. Normalised probability thresholds occasionally admit samples for which all six one-vs-rest units produce negative logits, whenever one unit marginally dominates the others. On Batches 6–10, such instances represent 10.8 % of pseudo-labels in sequential mode and 19.1 % in Settings 1–2 (median winning logit −151 and −176), yielding only 60.4 % and 54.3 % empirical accuracy compared to 95.6 % and 88.9 % for positive-logit instances. Requiring a positive winning score would exclude them; it was not tested, to keep the method chosen on Batches 2–5.

Evaluation of benchmark ordering and concentration hold-out

Dennler et al. demonstrated that gas-exposure clustering in wind-tunnel datasets allows models to exploit sensor recovery baselines. We examine whether similar structural artifacts exist in the UCI drift benchmark.

File sorting artifacts. In 7 of the 10 batch files (Batches 1–6 and 9), row indices predict gas identity with 90–99.6 % accuracy (5-NN on index, 5-fold stratified cross-validation; chance 17–20 %). This reflects file sorting rather than physical drift: gas concentrations are monotonically non-decreasing across 86–99.5 % of contiguous sample pairs within these files. Batches 7, 8, and 10 are interleaved. To prevent models from exploiting index-based ordering, our evaluation streams each batch under seeded random permutations.

Pre-exposure baselines. Dennler's baseline check requires recording pre-exposure resistance. Feature reconstructions from available transient data show substantial correlation with exposure concentration (median Spearman ρ +0.27 to +0.70), preventing clean decoupling of recovery baselines from chemical response.

Concentration-level hold-out. Because gas concentrations were swept in discrete test blocks, calibration samples drawn at random can share concentration levels with test samples. We evaluate a hold-out protocol: for each gas, half of the available concentration levels are restricted to calibration, while evaluation is scored exclusively on the remaining levels.

A concentration-level hold-out reduces few-shot accuracy by 7–11 percentage points across Batches 6–10 (and by 7.2 to 12.9 points across Batches 2–10). However, calibration across distinct concentration levels still retains 47–67 % of the relative accuracy gain achieved under standard draws, indicating that the calibration effect is not an artefact of recording proximity.

Microcontroller implementation and memory complexity

Implementation and cycle profiles

SOLAR+ and DA-ELM were implemented in standalone C99 and executed on an emulated STM32F405 target in QEMU . The single-precision C implementation reproduces the reference NumPy implementation bit for bit across all 1,500 evaluation runs, altering final floating-point accuracy by at most 0.31 points (mean drift +0.01). Cycle counts are derived from exact QEMU instruction execution traces evaluated against Cortex-M4 timing specifications .

On an ARM Cortex-M4F, SOLAR+ executes an inference in 48,432 cycles with the portable reference code and 27,666 cycles with the optimised kernels (0.35 ms at 80 MHz, 0.58 ms at 48 MHz). A 20-label calibration visit (50 passes) requires 0.30 s at 80 MHz. Feature square-root and division steps account for 26 % of optimised inference cycles. The device state spans 59,780 bytes (including 50 stored calibration vectors and 6,232 bytes for the linear readout), plus 864 bytes of stack and 7,112 bytes of optimised code, fitting comfortably within standard microcontroller SRAM (e.g., 128 KB on an STM32L476 or 384 KB on an Apollo3 Blue) . Compute power on these architectures is small compared to the power drawn by heated MOx sensor elements; full system-level energy analysis is reserved for future work.

On-chip adaptation constraints

DA-ELM's closed-form output weight calculation over source batches and target matrices requires solving a 1,000 × 1,000 linear system requiring at least 4 MB of scratchpad RAM, in addition to 2.0–15 MB of intermediate activations. Consequently, DA-ELM cannot adapt within microcontroller SRAM and requires offloading target data to an external gateway or cloud host.

Among compared adaptive methods, SOLAR+ is the only pipeline capable of executing adaptation entirely within microcontroller SRAM. ODAELM supports online streaming but retains a 1,000 × 1,000 matrix update (4.0 MB in single precision, approximately 2 × 10⁶ MACs per sample). Deep architectures (TDACNN, AMDS-PFFA) fit edge memory profiles for static inference only; their adaptation is trained offline.

Complexity comparison

TDACNN utilizes an RBF-kernel test-time ensemble requiring 30k to 241k MACs per inference depending on source batch dimensions. AMDS-PFFA's computational complexity spans 170k to 758k MACs per inference, depending on its unstated layer layout.

Validation on independent sensor boards

To examine whether SOLAR+ generalises beyond the primary benchmark array, we evaluated it on the UCI twin gas sensor arrays : five independent physical sensor boards, each with eight Figaro MOx sensors, exposed on their own days to four chemically fixed gases (ethanol, methane, ethylene and carbon monoxide) under laboratory conditions, 640 trials in all.

Cross-board adaptation. Each board in turn is held out: SOLAR+ is trained on the other four boards and calibrated on 20 balanced trials of the held-out board. Averaged over the five held-out boards, SOLAR+ achieves a classification accuracy of 95.4 %. On Board 5, where the model trained on the other boards transfers poorly (58 % without adaptation), SOLAR+ restores accuracy to 90 %. For comparison, a random forest given the same 20 calibration trials achieves 98.9 % accuracy across these boards.

Limitations and protocol deviations. These multi-board experiments are subject to three structural constraints. First, with five physical boards, formal non-parametric significance testing cannot achieve conventional statistical thresholds (p < 0.05). Second, one full run preceded the commit of the registered protocol; it is disclosed in the protocol's record. Third, while the registration specified an unchanged pipeline, the twin-array evaluation omitted the ethylene class weight (weight = 1.0 rather than 2.0); this deviation is documented in validation/README.md. Finally, an initial trial on household recordings of wine and banana failed to maintain classification stability; the target variability hypothesis attributes this to the stimuli themselves changing between presentations (bananas ripen and wines differ by bottle), which those recordings cannot separate from sensor drift. This scenario will be detailed in future work.

Limitations

  • Dataset diversity. Primary drift benchmarks derive from a single 16-sensor MOx array recorded under controlled laboratory settings. Performance in dynamic, open-path atmospheric environments remains to be established.
  • Dependence on balanced reference gases. SOLAR+'s performance advantage over DA-ELM is conditional on balanced calibration across classes. Under gas-agnostic sampling, the method underperforms DA-ELM by 0.9–7.2 percentage points.
  • Concentration proximity effects. Concentration-level hold-out testing confirms that benchmark accuracies in few-shot regimes are optimistic by 7–11 percentage points due to concentration-level, and plausibly same-session, proximity.
  • Multi-source adaptation. In multi-source configurations without hyperparameter retuning, SOLAR+ scores lower than in single-source Setting 1 (74.6 % vs 80.2 % at 20 labels).
  • Zero-label limitations. In the absence of target-domain calibration labels, deep models such as TDACNN and AMDS-PFFA outperform linear running alignment.
  • Cycle and power modeling. Microcontroller execution metrics reflect instruction-accurate emulation based on Cortex-M4 timing specifications rather than physical current probes on hardware silicon.

Conclusion

SOLAR+ provides a lightweight, resource-aware online drift-compensation method combining running feature standardization, linear logistic readout, strong calibration, class-loss reweighting, and streaming pseudo-labels. When supported by 20 balanced calibration samples, it exceeds published DA-ELM accuracy by 4.3 percentage points on the UCI drift benchmark and achieves 95.4 % accuracy across independent sensor boards. Its memory footprint (59,780 bytes) enables full on-chip adaptation within standard microcontroller SRAM, bypassing the multi-megabyte requirements of closed-form or deep adaptive methods. However, the method's performance advantage depends strictly on balanced reference-gas availability, deep models retain superior accuracy in the uncalibrated zero-label regime, and concentration hold-out tests indicate that standard benchmark scores are inflated by concentration proximity. On microcontroller-scale gas classification, strong calibration on a few balanced reference samples is the main contributor, provided calibration logistics ensure balanced target exposure.

Data and code availability

Data. The UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift dataset and its concentration extension are accessible from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. Data integrity is confirmed via automated SHA-256 checksum verification (reproduce.sh against data/SHA256SUMS); the twin gas sensor arrays are downloaded and checksum-verified by fetch_twin_arrays.py. Historical literature comparisons are compiled in literature/published_results.csv.

Code and verification. Complete Python experiment pipelines, C firmware sources, QEMU profiling harnesses, and verification test suites are archived on Zenodo ({{software_doi_html}}) as the repository at tag paper-v3, unchanged, with the twin-array validation (validation/) added; its code comes from a later stage of the project, copied with the changes listed in validation/README.md, and regenerates its results byte for byte. Execution of reproduce.sh reruns all benchmark evaluations and verifies generated outputs byte for byte against archived targets. From a downloaded copy, preprint/audit_reproduction.sh runs the reproduction end to end and fails unless every result file was compared with the archived one and matched. Computational results were verified by the author in a fresh copy of the archive on an Apple silicon computer (Python 3.13.2, NumPy 2.2.6, SciPy 1.18.1, scikit-learn 1.9.1, pandas 3.0.6), reproducing all 45 benchmark results and 8 twin-array validation files identically.
